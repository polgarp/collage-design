# Registers — ingredients for a graft

A movement is made by crossing **one collage lineage** with **one vernacular from outside
art**. The lineage brings the cut, the stacking and the craft bar, which is why the result looks
made. The vernacular brings a system (a grid, a notation, a sequence, a key), and in collage it
can also bring **the material itself**: real scanned seed catalogues, tide tables or fruit-crate
labels are as cuttable as any photograph. A lineage alone makes a pastiche, and a vernacular
alone makes a scrapbook.

These lists are starting points. Don't take the first entry that fits. Any lineage or vernacular
missing from here is welcome, and the further from the brief's obvious territory, the better,
provided the graft can still carry the brief's information and its material can actually be found.

Each entry names its **signature**: the few production facts that make it read as itself. Get
those right, look them up when unsure, and the parent is recognisable without being copied.
Lineages are traditions and periods, never a living artist's manner.

---

## Collage lineages: cut, stack and craft

| Lineage | Signature | The craft bar |
|---|---|---|
| **Dada photomontage** (1918–20s) | Violent scale collisions, machine parts grafted onto bodies, newsprint and type fragments, a white ground showing through | The collisions are deliberate and the scale jumps are large; type comes from real print, never set |
| **Constructivist photomontage** | Diagonal axis, red and black over halftone photography, crowds, knife-cut straight edges, heavy rules | A few angles repeated exactly; one spot colour cuts across every photo |
| **Surrealist engraving collage** (1920s–30s) | Victorian wood and steel engravings joined so the seam disappears; impossible scenes in one consistent line | Engraving line density matched across every join, so the seam can't be found |
| **Victorian scrap album** | Die-cut chromolithograph scraps, embossed and glossy, overlapping in dense sentimental heaps, gilt borders | The die-cut edge is crisp and slightly bevelled; the heap has a strict front-to-back order |
| **Cubist papier collé** (1912–14) | Flat planes of newspaper, wallpaper and faux-bois paper, charcoal lines drawn across the joins | Few pieces, all flat; line and paper argue about which one is the object |
| **Tsugi-gami** (Heian joined paper) | Dyed papers torn and joined along ragged boundaries into landscape bands, calligraphy over the top | The torn edges are long and continuous; colour steps are quiet; the writing runs across the seams |
| **Pop collage** (1950s–60s) | Glossy magazine advertising, consumer goods, cut-out figures in domestic interiors, saturated print colour | Halftone and print gloss kept intact; the cut follows the product's own outline |
| **Punk cut-and-paste** | Photocopied tabloid pages, ransom letters, tape, black toner, a pasted headline slashed across a face | Generation loss applied evenly; the toner black is one black |
| **Décollage** (torn posters, 1950s–60s) | Layers of billboard posters torn back through their strata; colour fragments from older pastings showing through | The tears reveal a real order of pasting: a newer layer never sits under an older one |
| **Typological grid** | Same subject, same angle, same light, in a strict grid of photographs | Every cell matched in scale and horizon; the grid itself is the reconciling move |
| **Scan / flatbed collage** | Objects laid straight on the glass, depth falloff, light leaking at edges, RGB fringe on movement | One scanner's light throughout; the falloff is consistent across the bed |

## Vernaculars: system and material

**As material** says whether open archives tend to hold it. *Yes* means it can be both the
structure and the fragments. *Structure only* means the format is usually copyrighted or
unavailable, so build the system and cut it from other material. *Check* means it depends on
country and date.

| Vernacular | Its system | As material | Where poster furniture lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Seed catalogue / packet** | Vignette, variety name, sowing-calendar bar, spacing diagram | Yes: USDA and BHL seed catalogues | Event months as the calendar bar |
| **Fruit-crate label** | Bold brand banner, a pictorial scene, the grower's town, a grade mark | Yes | Event name as the brand; venue as the grower's town |
| **Tide table / almanac** | Dense numeric columns, moon-phase glyphs, tiny caps headers | Yes: NOAA and old almanacs | Times and dates as tide entries |
| **Daily weather map** | Isobars, fronts, H/L cells, station plots | Yes: NOAA historical maps | Venue as the pressure centre |
| **Ship's log / ledger** | Ruled columns, handwritten entries, stamps and initials | Yes: NOAA and national archives | The event logged before it happens |
| **Topographic / survey map** | Contours, hatching, grid references, a legend | Yes: USGS and many national surveys | Venue by grid reference |
| **Star atlas** | Magnitude dots, constellation figures, coordinate grid | Yes: pre-1900 atlases | Acts as stars by size of billing |
| **Needlework pattern book** | Square grid, symbol key, row numbers | Yes: Internet Archive | Line-up in the symbol key |
| **Botanical key / plate** | Numbered couplets, specimen plate with figure numbers | Yes: BHL | "If you like X → stage 2"; acts as figures |
| **Sheet-music cover** | Big title lettering, a vignette, "as sung by", a price | Yes: pre-1929 covers | Headliner as "as sung by" |
| **Matchbox label** | Small framed vignette, ornamental border, maker's name, export line | Yes | Scaled up, the border holds the furniture |
| **Railway timetable** | Ruled table, dashed "does not stop", footnote symbols | Check: old public timetables usually yes | Line-up as stations; times as departures |
| **Postage stamp** | Perforations, denomination numeral, engraved portrait frame | Check: copyright varies by country | Date as the denomination |
| **Hazard placard** | Rotated squares, pictograms, UN numbers, strict colour codes | Check: many government designs are public domain | Warnings as the event's character |
| **Hand-painted shop sign** (West African, South Asian) | Painted lettering with drop shadows, an illustrated services list | Check: CC photographs exist | Activities as the "services" |
| **Papel picado** | Repeated cut-tissue banners, a symmetrical motif per sheet, strung in a row | Check: CC photographs; or cut the structure yourself | Each banner carries one fact |
| **Airline safety card** | Numbered panels, flat figures, arrows, red crosses | Structure only | A how-to sequence of the evening |
| **Flat-pack assembly sheet** | Wordless numbered steps, exploded views | Structure only | The event built step by step |
| **Thermal receipt** | Narrow monospace column, dotted rules, totals, barcode | Structure only, or the user's own | Programme as line items; price as the total |
| **Lotería card** | Numbered framed image, name banner | Structure only | One card per act |

## Pairing well

- **Distance makes the novelty, and contrast in register reads as wit.** A tender lineage with a
  bureaucratic vernacular (scrap album × ship's log), or a violent one with a gentle vernacular
  (Dada × seed catalogue). Two parents in the same mood merge into a blur.
- **Decide what the vernacular is for:** structure, material, or both. "Both" is the strongest
  graft and the most constrained, since the archive has to deliver. The philosophy says which.
- **Choose one from each list.** Two lineages make a mash-up; two vernaculars give a piece with
  no craft bar.
- **Name the graft in one phrase and test it.** "Décollage tide table" is good if no one has
  quite seen it before. If it names a look you can already picture exactly, push one parent
  further away.
